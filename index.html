1. Notice: your body already knows

Most of us were never asked what we want, so the answer isn’t in our heads yet. We start with your everyday life: the moments you feel lighter and more alive, and the ones that drain you. Your body notices before your mind can explain.

2. Imagine: picture the life you want

From those real moments, we picture a day in the life you want. It’s not a fantasy, it’s built from what already lights you up.

3. Practice: make it real, one step at a time

We name what could get in the way, make an if-then plan, and take one small step. See how it goes, adjust, try again.

The science behind it

Your body often knows first

In a well-known University of Iowa experiment, people played a card game with hidden risky decks. Their bodies showed small stress responses before they reached for a risky card, and they began choosing better before they could explain why. Scientists still debate exactly what this shows, but it suggests body signals carry real information. Noticing them is a skill called interoception, and it can be practiced.

Read the research: Bechara et al., the Iowa Gambling Task (summary) · Maia & McClelland, a critical look (PNAS)

Dreaming alone can drain you

Psychologist Gabriele Oettingen and colleagues found that people who only daydreamed about an ideal outcome had less energy afterward, measured by their blood pressure. In one study, people who fantasized about a great week went on to get fewer of their goals done.

Read the research: BPS summary · Kappes & Oettingen (2011)

Dream, then plan for the obstacle

What works better: picture what you want, name what’s in the way, then make an “if this happens, I’ll do that” plan. A 2021 review of 21 studies with nearly 16,000 people found this approach reliably helped people reach their goals.

Read the research: Wang, Wang & Gai (2021), Frontiers in Psychology

Solution-focused coaching

Our approach draws on solution-focused work, developed by Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg. Instead of digging into what’s wrong with you, we focus on the future you want and on what’s already working, then build from there.

The finish line keeps moving

Psychologists call it the hedonic treadmill. A new job, a raise, a promotion: each one feels great, then quickly becomes normal, and we start chasing the next thing. In a classic 1978 study, people who had won the lottery weren’t significantly happier than people who hadn’t.

Money helps, but less than we think

Money matters, especially when it’s tight. But a 2023 study found that for people who are unhappy, more income stops helping at around $100,000 a year. Heartbreak, loss, feeling lost: a bigger paycheck can’t fix those.

What the longest study of happiness found

Since 1938, Harvard researchers have followed people across their entire lives. One of their clearest findings: close relationships, more than money or fame, are what keep people happy and healthy.

School taught us how, never why

We learned how to get good grades, how to get into a good school, how to land the job. Nobody asked why. Why this path? What do I actually care about? It’s the big question most of us avoid, because it’s uncomfortable, and because the checklist kept us busy.

Why “why” changes everything

When you know your why, choices get clearer. You gain a quiet confidence that whatever happens, you’ll figure it out. And life stops feeling like a list of boxes to tick. You start enjoying the life you’re living, and the journey itself.
